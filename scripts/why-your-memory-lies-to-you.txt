In 1984, in North Carolina, a young woman named Jennifer Thompson was attacked in her home. During the attack, she did something remarkable. She forced herself to study her attacker's face. Every detail. She promised herself that if she survived, she would make sure he was caught.

Days later, she picked a man out of a photo lineup. Then again in a live lineup. In court, she pointed at him and said she was absolutely certain. His name was Ronald Cotton. He was sentenced to life in prison.

There was just one problem. Ronald Cotton was innocent.

More than ten years later, DNA evidence proved it was another man. Jennifer hadn't lied. She truly remembered Ronald's face. Her memory had been rewritten, and she never felt it happen.

So how can a memory that feels so real be so wrong? And how often is yours doing the same thing?

We tend to think of memory like a video camera. Something happens, the brain records it, and later we press play.

But that's not how memory works at all.

Back in 1932, psychologist Frederic Bartlett asked people to read an unfamiliar folk tale, then retell it later. Each time they retold it, the story changed. Strange details disappeared. Confusing parts were replaced with things that made more sense to them. And the people retelling it had no idea they were changing anything.

Bartlett realised something important. Remembering isn't replaying. It's rebuilding.

Every time you recall a memory, your brain reconstructs it from fragments, and fills in the gaps with what it expects, what it believes, and what it has heard since.

And this means memories can be changed from the outside.

In 1974, psychologists Elizabeth Loftus and John Palmer showed students films of car crashes. Then they asked one simple question. How fast were the cars going when they hit each other?

But for some students, they changed just one word. How fast were the cars going when they smashed into each other?

The students who heard "smashed" estimated higher speeds. And a week later, the researchers asked everyone whether they had seen any broken glass.

There was no broken glass in the film. But the "smashed" group was more than twice as likely to say they had seen it.

One word. And people remembered something that never happened.

Loftus wanted to know how far this could go. Could you plant an entire memory?

In the 1990s, she and Jacqueline Pickrell tried. They gave volunteers short stories about events from their childhood, supplied by their families. All were true except one. A story about getting lost in a shopping mall as a child, and being rescued by an elderly stranger.

None of the volunteers had ever been lost in a mall. But after a few interviews, about a quarter of them came to remember it. Some even added their own details. What the stranger looked like. How scared they felt.

A completely false childhood memory, built from a few sentences.

Now you might be thinking, sure, small details can blur. But I'd never forget something truly important.

Let's test that.

On the morning of January the 28th, 1986, the space shuttle Challenger exploded shortly after launch. The next day, psychologists Ulric Neisser and Nicole Harsch asked a group of students to write down exactly how they had heard the news.

Around two and a half years later, they asked the same students again.

Many of the stories had completely changed. Different places. Different people. Different ways of hearing the news. And here's the unsettling part. Many students were highly confident in their new version. When they were shown their own handwritten accounts from the day after, some insisted the new memory was the real one.

Psychologists call these flashbulb memories. They feel vivid and permanent, like a photograph. But research suggests they can fade and change much like ordinary memories. What stays strong is not the accuracy. It's the confidence.

And that brings us back to Jennifer Thompson.

After the attack, she saw Ronald Cotton's photo. Then she saw him in a lineup. Then she saw him again in court. Each time, his face was laid over the blurry memory of that night. And each time, she became more certain.

Her confidence wasn't proof that her memory was right. It may have been a sign of how many times it had been rewritten.

According to the Innocence Project, mistaken eyewitness identification played a role in around seven out of ten wrongful convictions later overturned by DNA evidence.

So why would your brain work this way? Why not just keep a perfect record?

One idea is that memory was never designed to be an archive. It was designed to help you survive the future. A flexible memory can blend old experiences with new information, so you can predict what will happen next and make better choices. Keeping the gist matters more than keeping every detail.

Some research also suggests that when you recall a memory, it can become temporarily changeable before it's stored again. Every remembering is a chance for something new to slip in.

Most of the time, that's helpful. But it means your memory is less like a recording, and more like a story you keep editing, without noticing the edits.

So what can you do about it?

First, write things down. If something matters, a conversation, an agreement, an event, record it as soon as you can. A note made on the day is usually far more reliable than a memory years later.

Second, be careful with confidence. Feeling sure is not the same as being right. When you and someone else remember an event differently, it's possible neither of you is lying. Both memories may have drifted.

And third, watch the words. Leading questions, retold stories and old photos can all reshape what you remember. If you want to know what really happened, ask open questions, and listen before you fill in the gaps.

As for Ronald Cotton and Jennifer Thompson? After he was freed, she asked to meet him, and apologised. He forgave her. They became friends, and wrote a book together about memory, mistakes and forgiveness.

Your memory is one of the most powerful things you have. It's who you are.

But it isn't a camera. It's a storyteller.
