It's Sunday. Six p.m. The weekend isn't over. You're still free. Nothing bad has happened. And yet, something in your chest starts to tighten. Tomorrow is Monday. The emails. The alarm. The meeting you've been avoiding. You're not even there yet, but your brain has already clocked in. People call it the Sunday scaries. And it's not just you. In one LinkedIn survey, around eight in ten professionals said they get it. So why does a day that hasn't happened yet ruin the one you're actually living? Let's start with a strange experiment. In 2006, neuroscientist Gregory Berns put people in a brain scanner and told them they were going to get a series of electric shocks. They could choose when. Some chose to take a stronger shock right away, rather than wait for a weaker one. Read that again. They chose more pain, just to stop waiting. Because for the brain, waiting for something bad can be its own kind of pain. Berns found that the parts of the brain that respond to pain were already active during the wait. The dread wasn't just a thought. It was a cost. And that's what Sunday night is. A long, slow wait for a shock called Monday. Your brain is built to look ahead. Thinking about the future helped our ancestors prepare for danger: the winter, the predator, the rival. But that same system can't always tell the difference between a lion and a Monday morning inbox. It sees a threat coming, and it starts preparing. Your heart rate rises. Your thoughts speed up. Your body gets ready for a fight that is still fifteen hours away.

And it gets worse, because Sunday has the perfect conditions for dread. First, the switch. On the weekend, you get to be yourself. On Monday, you have to become your work self again. Psychologists call the moment between those roles a transition, and transitions are hard. Your brain is packing up one version of you, and unpacking another. Second, the unfinished business. In the 1920s, psychologist Bluma Zeigarnik noticed that waiters remembered unpaid orders perfectly, but forgot them the moment the bill was paid. Unfinished tasks stick in the mind. They nag. That's why, on Sunday night, every email you didn't reply to suddenly comes back. Your brain is holding a to-do list it can't put down. Third, the uncertainty. Studies on anxiety have found that not knowing what's coming can feel worse than knowing something bad is coming. Will the boss be in a mood? Will the meeting go wrong? Your brain hates a blank, so it fills it in. Usually with the worst version. And fourth, the clock. The weekend is ending, and you can see it ending. So instead of enjoying Sunday, you spend it mourning it. You lose the last hours of your weekend, worrying about losing your weekend. But here's the twist. Monday is usually not that bad. In 2012, researchers Arthur Stone and colleagues looked at the moods of hundreds of thousands of Americans. Yes, people were happier on the weekend. But Monday wasn't the worst day at all. It was roughly the same as Tuesday, Wednesday and Thursday. The famous Monday blues were mostly a myth. This fits something psychologists Daniel Gilbert and Timothy Wilson call the impact bias. We are terrible at predicting how bad things will feel. We imagine the future as worse, and longer, than it really is. Which means Sunday night isn't really about Monday. It's about the Monday your brain made up.

So how do you get your Sunday back? First, close the loops. In 2011, researchers E. J. Masicampo and Roy Baumeister found that unfinished goals stopped nagging people once they made a specific plan for them. You don't have to do the task. You just have to write down when you will. So on Friday, before you leave, write tomorrow's first three jobs. Your brain can let go, because it knows the list is safe. Second, give the worry a time slot. Psychologist Thomas Borkovec found that setting aside a short, fixed worry time each day helped people worry less overall. So give yourself ten minutes on Sunday afternoon. Worry on purpose. Then, when the dread comes back at nine p.m., tell it: "We already did this." Third, put something good on Monday. A coffee you love. Lunch with a friend. A small thing to look forward to. Dread is about the future, so give your future something worth waiting for. Fourth, protect Sunday evening. Don't check work email. Not even a quick look. One message can pull your whole brain back into work mode. And fifth, if the dread is huge every single week, listen to it. Sometimes the Sunday scaries are just a brain doing its job. But sometimes, they're a message. That something about your work, or your life, needs to change. Because here's the truth. Your brain isn't trying to ruin your weekend. It's trying to protect you from a Monday that, most of the time, never arrives. The monster under the bed is mostly made of shadows. So this Sunday, when the dread starts, remember. You're not in Monday yet. You're still here. And the weekend is still yours. So, what time does your Sunday dread usually start? Tell me in the comments.
